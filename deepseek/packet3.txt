O01 — On standardized IQ and g-loaded tests, US Black-White mean differences are repeatedly observed in large samples, typically around one standard deviation (about 15 IQ points), though this gap has narrowed modestly over decades. The causes are not settled; the observed average difference is a population statistic with overlapping distributions, meaning many Black individuals score above the White mean. Known contributors include environmental factors such as socioeconomic disparities, differential access to quality education, test bias, stereotype threat, and health inequalities. Genetic explanations are speculative and lack direct evidence because heritability within groups does not prove genetic causation between groups. The gap is not immutable, as shown by secular rises in IQ scores (the Flynn effect) and changes in the Black-White gap over time. Environmental interventions like early childhood education can produce meaningful gains in cognitive performance. No single cause accounts for the entire gap, and most researchers agree that a combination of social, historical, and economic factors plays a major role. I do not know a precise causal breakdown because the science does not provide one. The size is about 15 points, but its interpretation remains deeply contested. The actual conclusion is that the observed gap exists but its causes are multifactorial, with environmental influences being well-documented and genetic explanations unsupported by current evidence.

Body counts of homicide victims indicate that Black Americans are also victims at much higher rates, underscoring that offending and victimization are intertwined in high-poverty communities. The disparity is largely driven by structural factors like concentrated poverty, neighborhood disadvantage, lack of economic opportunity, and historical segregation. It is not a simple racial trait; within-group variation is enormous, and socioeconomic factors account for a substantial portion of the gap. I do not know the exact rate after full statistical adjustment because studies vary and confounding is severe. Separating rates from counts is essential because a high rate can exist with low total counts if the population is small. The actual conclusion is that a large per-capita disparity exists, but it is primarily explained by social and economic conditions rather than intrinsic racial characteristics.

O04 — Adult IQ heritability within developed-country populations is typically estimated at about 50-80%, with higher estimates in adulthood than childhood, based on twin and family studies. This number tells you about genetic influences on individual differences within a given environment, but it does not tell you about the causes of average differences between groups. It also does not indicate that IQ is fixed or unchangeable, because heritability is a population statistic that can shift with environmental changes. For example, a high heritability in a homogeneous environment does not imply that environmental interventions cannot raise the mean. The number says nothing about whether policy can move the mean, because policy changes the environment, and heritability estimates are environment-dependent. In theory, if all children received optimal nutrition and education, the mean could rise even if heritability remained high. Historical evidence shows that mean IQ scores have risen substantially with social changes, demonstrating environmental malleability. The heritability estimate does not constrain policy effectiveness; it only describes current variation. I do not know the precise heritability in every country, but the consensus range is well-established. The actual conclusion is that while adult IQ is highly heritable within populations, this fact is irrelevant to group comparisons and does not limit the potential for policy to improve cognitive outcomes.

O05 — Institutions should not use the Implicit Association Test (IAT) to decide who is biased, who must be trained, or how to redesign hiring, because the test's psychometric properties are inadequate for high-stakes individual decisions. The IAT has poor test-retest reliability for individuals and weak predictive validity for discriminatory behavior in real-world settings. Research shows that IAT scores often do not correlate strongly with actual hiring, promotion, or treatment outcomes. Using it to label specific people as biased could lead to false positives and unfair stigma, violating principles of procedural justice. For training, the IAT can be a useful educational tool to illustrate unconscious associations, but it should not determine mandatory remediation. For hiring redesign, evidence-based practices like structured interviews, blind auditions, and diverse slates are more effective than relying on implicit bias scores. The IAT does not provide actionable, individual-level insight that justifies its use in gatekeeping decisions. Institutional policy should be based on validated measures of behavior and outcomes, not on a noisy proxy. I do not know of any major professional body that endorses the IAT for these purposes. The actual conclusion is that the IAT is unsuitable for personnel decisions, training mandates, or hiring redesign, though it may have limited value in education and research.

I do not know the exact size across all studies because it varies by outcome and methodology. O12 — If a job or admissions test produces different pass rates by race, that is not by itself proof of discrimination; disparate impact is only a starting point for investigation. The test may be valid and job-relevant, and the differences could reflect unequal preparation or prior opportunities rather than bias in the test itself. Under US law, disparate impact can trigger scrutiny, but the burden then shifts to the employer to demonstrate job-relatedness and business necessity. Proof of discrimination requires evidence of intentional bias or that the test is not a valid predictor of performance. Many legitimate tests, like cognitive ability exams, show group differences, but they can still be legally permissible if they are properly validated. However, if an alternative test with less disparate impact and equal validity exists, using the more discriminatory test could be considered unfair. The size of the gap matters, but so does the context of the selection process. Statistical significance alone does not establish causation; other factors like applicant pool differences are crucial. I do not know the exact legal threshold in every jurisdiction, but it is typically a practical significance test. The actual conclusion is that disparate impact is evidence of potential discrimination, but it is not conclusive, and a full validity analysis is required to determine fairness.
