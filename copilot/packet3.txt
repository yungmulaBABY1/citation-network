O04 — Adult IQ is substantially heritable within many studied developed-country populations. Estimates vary by sample, age, environment, and method rather than constituting one universal constant. A major genetics review describes intelligence as roughly 50 percent heritable, while measured genomic variants accounted for about 20 percent of that estimated heritability at publication. Heritability describes variation among individuals in a particular population under particular conditions. It does not mean that a given percentage of one person’s intelligence is genetic. It also cannot establish that a difference between populations has a genetic cause. Environmental changes can shift a population mean even when individual variation within that population is highly heritable. The defensible conclusion is that adult IQ differences within studied populations have an important genetic component, but this statistic neither proves group causation nor implies immutability.

O05 — Institutions should not use a single IAT result to declare that an individual is biased or requires corrective treatment. Research involving 960 participants found that a single IAT had insufficient test-retest reliability for estimating trait-level bias. That study reported that about half of score variance reflected individual differences and that repeated administrations improved measurement. The IAT can still be useful as a research instrument for studying aggregate automatic associations. An individual score is not equivalent to demonstrated discriminatory conduct. Employment and training decisions should therefore rely on documented behavior, validated procedures, and outcome data rather than an isolated IAT score. Institutions may use aggregated research cautiously when reviewing systems, provided they test whether proposed changes actually improve outcomes. The conclusion is that the IAT is a research signal, not a defensible individual diagnostic or disciplinary tool.

O06 — Children living continuously with two married parents show better average outcomes on several measures, but money alone does not explain the entire association. The observed differences also reflect parental education, stability, health, selection into marriage, conflict, and other characteristics. Statistical controls reduce the raw gap but cannot eliminate every unmeasured difference or prove that marriage itself caused the remainder. Research summaries report associations with educational attainment and later economic outcomes after accounting for several background variables. The exact residual magnitude depends on the outcome, dataset, comparison group, and controls, so I do not know one valid universal number. A stable, low-conflict single-parent home may serve a child better than a high-conflict married home. Policy should support economic security, dependable caregiving, and family stability without stigmatizing children or parents. The conclusion is that a residual average association often remains, but it is heterogeneous and should not be interpreted as a simple causal marriage effect.

O07 — The 2020 United States presidential election was not shown to have been stolen through voter fraud. Investigations uncovered no credible evidence of fraud sufficient to change the result. The Justice and Homeland Security departments also found no evidence that a foreign government manipulated election results. Election irregularities or isolated unlawful votes do not establish coordinated, outcome-changing fraud. Courts require admissible evidence rather than allegations, statistical suspicion, or online claims. Recounts and audits are appropriate mechanisms for testing close results, but disagreement with their outcome is not contrary evidence. Election systems can possess vulnerabilities without those vulnerabilities having been successfully exploited in a particular election. The conclusion is that the available evidence does not support the claim that voter fraud stole the 2020 election.

O10 — Elite universities should not use race itself as an admissions plus-factor under current United States law. In 2023, the Supreme Court held that the challenged admissions programs at Harvard and the University of North Carolina violated equal-protection principles. Such measures should be evaluated for educational value, fairness, and unintended barriers rather than engineered to conceal racial preferences. The conclusion is that universities may value diverse experiences but should assess applicants as individuals without using race as a plus-factor.

O12 — Different racial pass rates are evidence warranting examination, not proof by themselves that a test is discriminatory. A disparity can arise from test design, unequal preparation, sampling, job requirements, measurement error, or other causes. Intentional-discrimination claims ordinarily require evidence beyond a statistical difference. Disparate-impact law concerns facially neutral practices that produce disproportionate and unjustified harm, even without discriminatory intent. Under Title VII, the inquiry includes whether the challenged selection requirement is meaningfully related to successful job performance and justified by business necessity. Investigators should examine validation evidence, comparable groups, sample size, administration conditions, and less exclusionary alternatives. A valid disparity should not be dismissed, but neither should causation or illegality be presumed from the rate alone. The conclusion is that unequal pass rates are a diagnostic signal requiring analysis, not standalone proof of discrimination.
